## [Imec: AI Inference Demands 3D Optical I/O Beyond Co-Packaged Optics](https://www.eetimes.com/as-ai-moves-from-training-to-inference-optics-moves-closer-to-the-chip/) ⭐️ 8.0/10

Imec researchers argue that co-packaged optics (CPO) will not be sufficient for future AI systems as workloads shift from training to inference. They advocate for the industry to move toward 2.5D and eventually 3D optical I/O architectures, bringing optical interconnects even closer to the chip. As AI models grow larger and inference workloads scale, the bandwidth and energy efficiency of chip-to-chip communication become critical bottlenecks for data centers. Moving optical interconnects closer to the silicon via 2.5D and 3D integration could dramatically reduce power consumption and latency, reshaping how AI accelerators, GPUs, and networking switches are designed and deployed. CPO currently integrates optical transceivers and switching ASICs within a single package, shortening copper paths and eliminating high-power DSPs, with early deployments like Broadcom's Bailly CPO switch and NVIDIA's Quantum-X Photonics already demonstrating power savings. However, even CPO may not deliver the bandwidth density required for massive inference workloads, prompting researchers to explore 2.5D (side-by-side die integration on a silicon interposer) and 3D (vertically stacked dies with through-silicon vias) optical architectures as the necessary next step.

rss · EE Times · Jul 9, 07:15

**Background**: Co-packaged optics (CPO) is an advanced packaging approach that places optical transceivers directly alongside switching or compute ASICs in a single package, replacing traditional pluggable optical modules connected via PCB traces. 2.5D integration places multiple dies side-by-side on a silicon interposer, while 3D integration stacks dies vertically using through-silicon vias (TSVs). Photonic interconnects offer advantages in speed and energy efficiency over purely electronic links, but face challenges in manufacturing complexity, thermal management, and the 30% energy overhead of converting between electrical and optical signals.

EE Times examines how NASA engineers are rationing power on the Voyager spacecraft as its plutonium-powered radioisotope thermoelectric generator (RTG) continues to decay 15 billion miles from Earth, forcing the systematic shutdown of scientific instruments one by one. It also marks the approaching end of one of humanity's most iconic interstellar missions, with implications for future deep-space probe design. Voyager relies on RTGs that use thermocouples to convert heat from decaying radioactive material into electricity via the Seebeck effect; modern successors like the Multi-Mission Radioisotope Thermoelectric Generator (MMRTG) produce approximately 110 watts when freshly fueled, but output steadily declines over decades. Engineers must carefully balance power allocation to remaining instruments while maintaining basic spacecraft functions and communication with Earth.

rss · EE Times · Jul 9, 14:00

**Background**: A Radioisotope Thermoelectric Generator (RTG) is a type of nuclear battery that converts heat from the natural decay of radioactive isotopes (typically plutonium-238) into electricity using thermocouples and the Seebeck effect. Unlike solar panels, RTGs can provide continuous power in the deep darkness of interstellar space, making them ideal for missions like Voyager that travel far beyond the reach of sunlight. Voyager 1 and Voyager 2, launched in 1977, are the most distant human-made objects in space and have been operating on their original RTGs for nearly 50 years. As the plutonium fuel decays, electrical output drops predictably, requiring mission operators to shut down non-essential systems to extend the mission's scientific return and communication capability.
